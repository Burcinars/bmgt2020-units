The Way We Do Things Around Here

Organizational culture: the rules nobody writes down. Plus film week, with a company that asks for everything.

Your first day at a new job. Someone hands you the employee handbook. Dress code, page 12. Time off, page 19. Values, page 3. You will skim it once and never open it again.

The real rules arrive another way. You learn them by watching. What happens to the person who disagrees with the boss? Who eats lunch together? Does "my door is always open" survive a bad week? Nobody wrote these rules down. Everyone follows them. By week three, you will too.

Those unwritten rules are organizational culture. This unit teaches you to see it, read it, and shape it. You will visit two coffee shops with the same handbook and opposite personalities. You will watch a CEO rewrite his company's culture in one week. You will study a bank whose culture quietly paid people to cheat. Then comes film week: The Circle, a company whose culture asks for everything.

Part 1 · The Invisible Rulebook

Two rulebooks

Picture your first month at a new company. Orientation gave you the official rules: hours, dress code, and a values page that says "we value open communication."

Three weeks later, you know a second set of rules. No document lists them:

🕰 Meetings start four minutes late. Always. 🤐 Never disagree with Dana in front of a client. 🚪 Leaving at 5:00 sharp is allowed, but it costs you. ☕ Bad news goes to the coffee machine first, not the form.

Nobody taught you these rules on purpose. You picked them up by watching. Human beings are experts at spotting what a group really rewards. That second rulebook has a name.

Key term

Organizational culture: the shared values, beliefs, and unwritten rules that shape how people actually behave. The company's personality.

Culture is the way we do things around here. The oldest definition in the field. Used by McKinsey's Marvin Bower in the 1960s, made famous by Deal and Kennedy in 1982.

Read it again, slowly. Not the way we SAY we do things. The way we DO them.

🎨 Four color-coded words

This unit color-codes four little words. They will keep the same colors on every page:

SEE  what you can watch happening

SAY  what the company claims

DO  what people actually do

BELIEVE  what everyone assumes, without saying it

Culture lives in the space between SAY and DO. Keep that one sentence. It carries the whole unit.

Part 1 · The Invisible Rulebook

You can't see it. You can feel it.

You cannot point at a culture. It is invisible. But you can feel it fast, because it decides three things in every job:

How decisions really get made. In the meeting? Or before the meeting, between two people?

Who feels safe speaking up. Can the newest person say "I think this is wrong"?

What happens after bad news. The first sixty seconds after "we have a problem" tell you almost everything.

Researchers describe a strong, healthy culture as a quiet guide: it lines hundreds of people up behind the same goals, with no orders needed (Groysberg, Lee, Price, & Cheng, 2018).

One warning before we go on. Culture is not the perks. A ping-pong table is furniture. Free snacks are groceries. The real test is this: does your teammate feel safe telling the boss the launch is broken? Some of the most brutal workplaces on earth have beautiful snack walls. This week's film is about that exact trick.

A fact-check footnote 🧐

You will hear this quote in every culture talk forever: "Culture eats strategy for breakfast." It is always credited to management legend Peter Drucker.

Here is the thing. Researchers went looking, and there is no evidence Drucker ever said it. The line spread in the 2000s because it feels true and everyone repeats it.

Now notice what that is: a story spreading through a group, with nobody checking it. That is culture, in the wild. In this unit, claims come with sources. Keep the habit.

Part 2 · The Iceberg

Schein's iceberg: three layers deep

The person who mapped culture's anatomy was Edgar Schein of MIT. He spent fifty years asking one question: how do groups come to believe what they believe? His answer is the field's standard model, and it looks like an iceberg (Schein, 2010).

Three layers. The deeper you go, the harder it is to see, and the more power it has.

Level 1 · Artifacts · SEE

Everything you can see and hear: the office layout, how people dress, the slogans on the wall, the rituals, the jargon, who talks in meetings.

Level 2 · Espoused values · SAY

What the organization says it values: mission statements, the values slide, the careers page.

Level 3 · Underlying assumptions · BELIEVE

The beliefs so deep that nobody says them out loud. People here just "know" them. Deepest layer, strongest pull, hardest to change.

Watch one example run through all three layers at a tech startup. The snack wall and open floor plan: you can SEE them. The all-hands slide "collaboration is everything": the company SAYS it. And the unspoken rule that working alone with headphones on looks lazy: everyone BELIEVES it. Nobody ever voted on that third one. It just runs the room.

Part 2 · The Iceberg

The gap that tells the truth

Now put two of our color words side by side. What a company SAYS and what its people DO can drift far apart. The space between them is the most honest data in any building.

The wall SAYS

"We practice radical candor. Bad news travels fast here. We celebrate failure as learning."

The meeting DOES

Silence when the boss proposes something risky. Problems dressed up as "challenges." Real opinions traded in the hallway, after.

When the wall and the meeting disagree, nobody believes the wall. Netflix said this perfectly in its famous 2009 culture deck: a company's actual values are shown by who gets rewarded, promoted, or let go. That one sentence beats most books on this topic.

Why the gap matters

The gap is rarely a lie. It is what pressure does to nice words, one small compromise at a time. But employees feel the gap as hypocrisy, and hypocrisy is expensive: it teaches people that the official channel is theater.

Your job as a future manager: shrink the gap. Your job as a job-seeker, starting now: read the gap before you sign.

Part 3 · Where Culture Comes From

Nobody installs a culture

No company has ever held a meeting called "let's choose our underlying assumptions." Culture grows. It has three main roots:

The founders' beliefs. Whatever the first leaders reward and ignore in year one becomes "how we do things" by year five. A founder who personally answers every complaint plants one kind of company. A founder who guards the pitch deck plants another.

What worked. Schein's deepest idea, in plain words: culture is made of old wins. A solution works, so people repeat it. Then they teach it to newcomers as "the right way." After enough years, nobody remembers it was ever a choice.

Crises and scars. Organizations remember pain. A company that nearly died from one bad contract will treat every future contract like a bomb, decades after anyone remembers why.

Week 1 callback 🧵

You have already watched a culture being born. In the Hawthorne bank wiring room, fourteen workers invented their own rule for a fair day's work. Work too fast and you were a "rate buster." Too slow, a "chiseler." They enforced the rule with mockery and a punch on the arm.

No manager wrote that rule. Culture grows wherever a group solves problems together. The only question is whether it grows on purpose or by accident.

Part 3 · Where Culture Comes From

You were being socialized before day one

Key term

Socialization: the process where newcomers absorb the culture and become insiders.

It starts earlier than you think, and it ends when the rules stop feeling like rules:

The job ad's tone, the interview questions, the office you toured: all of it was teaching you what this tribe values, before you were hired. Then onboarding turns the volume up. Around month three, the finish line: the culture is now in you.

Companies with strong cultures refuse to leave this to chance. One went to an extreme that made the business press blink:

The Offer 💸

Near the end of new-hire training, the online shoe retailer Zappos offered every trainee about $2,000 to quit on the spot. Take the money, no hard feelings, goodbye.

The logic is simple culture math. If a few weeks of pay can pull you away, you were here for a paycheck, not for this place. That mismatch costs far more than $2,000 later. And everyone who turns the money down has just told themselves, publicly: I choose this culture. Amazon liked the idea enough to keep a version after buying Zappos in 2009.

The fine print

Hiring for "culture fit" has a shadow. Push it too hard and it becomes hiring for sameness: a company full of people who all think alike. The healthier target: match on values (how we treat customers, how we handle truth), and stay different in everything else.

Keep this tension in mind. This week's film opens with a job interview that is really a fit test, and Part 5 shows what too much sameness costs.

Part 4 · The Leader's Shadow

Your team reads you like a weather report

Here is the part of culture a manager can actually hold in their hands. Leaders broadcast culture all day, whether they mean to or not. Long before anyone reads your values memo, your team has studied what you DO, and copied it (Groysberg et al., 2018).

Edgar Schein mapped the channels leaders broadcast through. Five of them do most of the work. Notice that none of them is a speech.

What you measure. Whatever you keep asking about, people decide, must be what really matters here.

How you react when things go wrong. Crises are culture's classroom. One blowup at a messenger teaches a whole floor to hide problems.

What you reward, and who you promote. A promotion is the loudest values statement a company ever makes. Everyone studies the winner.

What you model. Your calendar. Your tone. Your 11 PM emails. "Do as I say" has never once beaten "do as I do."

Who you hire, and who you let go. Every arrival and every exit gets read as a definition of who belongs here.

Try it on a small example. A leader SAYS "we value work-life balance," then emails the team at midnight. What did the team learn? That the words are decoration. When leaders walk the talk, trust grows. When they do not, people quietly follow the walk and ignore the talk.

Two real cases, two directions. First: a culture rewritten in public, in days.

Part 4 · The Leader's Shadow

Case 1: rewriting a culture in one public week

In April 2022, Elon Musk, already running Tesla and SpaceX, offered about $44 billion for Twitter. The deal closed that October. What happened next became a live experiment: how fast can one leader's signals overwrite a culture?

Mass layoffs came first. Then an ultimatum to the survivors: commit to an "extremely hardcore" way of working, long hours at high intensity, or leave.

Then came the forty-eight hours every management class now studies. Musk tweeted that the app was slow and blamed how it was built. Eric Frohnhoefer, an engineer who had spent about six years building Twitter's Android app, corrected him. In public: "I have spent ~6yrs working on Twitter for Android and can say this is wrong."

Screenshots: CBS News MoneyWatch (Ivanova, November 15, 2022).

"He's fired." Two words, posted publicly, casual as a lunch order. Days later, reports said about twenty more engineers were let go for criticizing Musk internally (Ivanova, 2022; Diaz, 2022).

Now run the five signals. What got measured? Loyalty and intensity. What happened to bad news? The messenger was fired, in front of the world. Who was let go? The people who spoke. Every remaining employee learned the new rule in two days, and nobody needed a memo: disagreeing in public ends careers here.

Twitter's old culture had prized open internal debate (Stokel-Walker, 2022). The new one went quiet. Some people rallied to the mission. Many others stopped talking or left. And "stopped talking" is the part that should worry a manager most, because it means the next problem arrives late, or never.

Two honest readings 🧐

Do not file this as a simple villain story. Demanding, mission-driven cultures do ship. At Tesla, Musk slept on the factory floor during production crunches. That modeled total commitment, and some people do their best work inside that intensity. In July 2023 Twitter was rebranded to X: the outside name changed to match the new inside culture.

The management question is never "is intensity good or bad." It is: what does each signal buy, and what does it cost? A culture where correcting the boss is fatal buys speed and obedience. It pays in silence, burnout, and departures. Just know what you are purchasing.

Part 4 · The Leader's Shadow

Case 2: the same tools, pointed the other way

Howard Schultz grew up in Brooklyn public housing. When he was a kid, his father, a delivery driver, broke his ankle, lost his job, and had no insurance. Schultz watched his family fall. That memory became company design.

When Schultz built Starbucks into a giant, he loaded the five signals with the opposite values. Health benefits for part-time baristas, decades before that was normal. Stock grants called Bean Stock. Free college tuition through Arizona State. And one deliberate word carrying the whole worldview: employees are called partners.

What gets modeled? In 2017, Schultz stood at ASU's graduation and told the Starbucks partners in the crowd: "your station in life does not define you." You can watch him teach the company's values in public: Schultz's message to graduating partners (YouTube).

Week 1 callback 🧵

Recognize this playbook? It is Mayo's discovery, run at global scale: belonging, attention, and feeling valued drive performance. Week 1 gave you the theory. Starbucks is the theory with a profit-and-loss statement.

No halos

Keep your detective's eye anyway. In recent years, Starbucks has faced union drives and public fights over whether the partner promise is fully kept. That does not erase the culture. It proves the Part 2 lesson: the gap between SAY and DO is always the thing to watch, at every company, forever.

Musk and Schultz used the same five power tools: measuring, reacting, rewarding, modeling, hiring. Different values, opposite companies. Now the tools are yours. Let's see what you do with them.

Schein calls these embedding mechanisms. Your team calls them "how it really works here." You run them every day. The only choice is whether you run them on purpose.

Part 5 · Strong Medicine

Strong cultures and weak ones

Key term

A strong culture: core values that are widely shared and deeply held. Nearly everyone finishes the sentence "around here, we…" the same way, and means it.

In a weak culture, that sentence gets ten different endings. How things go depends on which manager you happen to get, and the formal rulebook has to do all the work.

Week 1 callback 🧵

Remember Weber? He designed bureaucracy to make behavior predictable through written rules. A strong culture does the same job without the paperwork: people just know what to do, because the values live inside them. That is why strong cultures feel smooth and fast.

It is also the danger. A written rule can be read, questioned, and appealed. An invisible rule cannot even be pointed at.

Strength buys real things, and every one of them has a shadow:

The bright side

Alignment without supervision. Speed: fewer debates. A proud, clear identity. Newcomers who know how to act by week two.

The shadow

Alignment becomes conformity. Speed becomes nobody pausing to question. Identity becomes us versus them. And a culture everyone fits is a culture nobody challenges.

How bad can the shadow get? Reader, meet a bank.

Part 5 · Strong Medicine

Anatomy of a disaster: Wells Fargo

Through the 2000s and 2010s, Wells Fargo's branch banking arm ran one of the strongest cultures in American business. It was organized around one gospel: cross-selling. The target: every customer household should hold eight Wells Fargo products. Why eight? The CEO later admitted, in essence: because it rhymed with "great."

Branches got daily sales targets, checked several times a day, with rankings, bonuses, and public shaming attached. Miss your numbers and you stayed late, calling your own family members. Keep missing and you were gone. Meanwhile, the values posters said what posters always say: customers first, integrity always.

Under that pressure, thousands of employees started opening accounts and credit cards that customers never asked for. They forged signatures and invented email addresses to do it. When the scandal broke in 2016, the first official story was: bad apples.

~3.5Maccounts eventually estimated as potentially unauthorized: opened without customers asking
5,300employees fired over the years for these sales practices
$3Bsettlement with the Justice Department and SEC in 2020, on top of earlier fines
The autopsy

Run the iceberg. The bank SAID: customers first. You could SEE: daily target boards, rankings, "eight is great." And everyone came to BELIEVE the real rule: the number is your job, and missing it gets you fired.

5,300 people did not independently wake up dishonest. The system made cheating the path of least resistance, and the values posters were wallpaper on top of it. Week 1 callback: Taylor taught you that incentives are power tools. Point them at a bad target, inside a culture too scared to speak, and this is what they cut.

The missing ingredient has a name

Very tight groups can slide into groupthink: protecting agreement instead of truth. Psychologist Irving Janis named that pattern in 1972. The antidote you already know from Week 1: psychological safety (Amy Edmondson's term), the shared confidence that speaking up is safe. It was Google's Project Aristotle finding, too.

Memorize the formula: strong culture + safe dissent. Strength without voice is how banks end up on the news.

Part 6 · Inside The Circle

Scene study 1: the interview

The film opens with Mae's job interview, and the room speaks before anyone does. No closed office. No desk between them. A bright, glassy, open lobby with a reception-style bar. That is an artifact you can SEE, and it is talking: we are transparent, we are modern, we are not bosses and workers here.

Whether that is true is a separate question. The film spends two hours on it.

Now watch what the interview is for. A normal interview gathers facts about the candidate. This one barely bothers. It is a fit test, and it is also socialization happening live: the interviewer teaches Mae the company's rhythm while measuring how fast she adopts it. Four moves to watch for:

The "grandma is falling asleep" move. Mid-answer, the interviewer signals that Mae's explanation is boring. The rule being taught: short, fun answers win. Long, careful answers lose. Will Mae accept that rule without pushing back?

The speed round. Rapid-fire questions, forced choices, no time to think. The answers barely matter. The real measure: is Mae comfortable being directed, at the company's pace?

The receptionist question. "What was the name of the person at the front desk?" On the surface: an attention check. Underneath: does Mae treat every small moment as something being graded? At this company, that instinct is the job.

The TruYou speech. The company's product, one identity for your whole life, is pitched as a belief: messy private life should become simple, open, unified. The interviewer watches: does Mae nod along? Or does she ask what gets lost when everything becomes "simple"?

Student takeaway

One sentence: the interviewer's real question is will Mae adopt the company's rhythm, language, and beliefs without questioning them? Remember the Zappos Offer: both companies filter hard for fit. The difference is what the culture does with you after you fit.

Part 6 · Inside The Circle

Scene study 2: Dream Friday

Every week, the whole campus fills an auditorium for Dream Friday, and Eamon Bailey presents. You know this from Part 3: that is a ritual, and rituals teach what matters. Watch Bailey work the room.

He enters holding a coffee mug. Sweater, not suit. He opens with a joke about himself and a little surf story. None of that is an accident. It is friendliness doing a job: an audience that finds you familiar does not question you. Notice that he never sells the product first. He sells himself as trustworthy.

Then the reveal: SeeChange. Tiny cameras, already placed all over the world, streaming live. People in those feeds never agreed to be filmed. And here is the masterstroke: his voice never changes. Calm body, warm tone, while announcing cameras everywhere. The audience borrows his calm. The hidden message: if this were dangerous, I would look worried. I don't. So it must be fine.

Moral reframing. Listen to the words. Never surveillance. Never monitoring. Instead: sharing, caring, accountability, safety, human rights. Once the idea is framed as ethical, questioning it feels unethical. You are not careful anymore. You are selfish.

The echo. Bailey gets the crowd to chant "Sharing is caring." Saying a belief out loud, together, does something to people: disagreeing later feels like breaking a promise. Participation creates commitment.

Soft hierarchy. New employees are lovingly called "guppies." It sounds sweet. It also files them under small, in a pond he owns. Power that never needs to raise its voice.

What is never said. No consent. No limits. No "what if this is misused?" The most important part of the speech is the missing part. By the time anyone thinks of the hard questions, the cheering is over and the feeling has already won.

Student takeaway

This is culture being manufactured live: a ritual, a hero on stage, loaded language, and a room trained to chant together. Connect Part 5: an auditorium that chants together is a room where the voice gauge reads near zero.

Part 6 · Inside The Circle

Scene study 3: "It's not mandatory…"

The scene every student remembers. Two smiling coworkers, Gina and a teammate, come to Mae's desk to talk about her weekend absence and her thin social profile. Nobody gives an order. Nobody raises a voice. Everyone stays warm. Watch it in slow motion, because this is soft power doing surgery.

"You have time?" is not a question. Gina is already standing at Mae's screen, already mid-task. The decision was made before Mae was asked. You will meet this move in real meetings forever: "Oh, quick thing…" quietly removes your exits.

Resistance becomes "misunderstanding." Mae explains she has been overwhelmed. She is protecting one small piece of space for herself. The answer: "That's so interesting that you put it that way." Sounds kind. It is a pivot. Mae's choice gets redefined as confusion: participation is not extra, it is part of the job. And once it is part of the job, opting out looks like a character flaw.

Compliments as anchors. "Everyone really likes you." "Your work has been exemplary." These are not gifts. They are leverage. Once you are labeled liked and successful, saying no puts that label at risk. It is hard to resist while being praised. That is exactly why the praise is there.

Surveillance, mentioned casually. "You left campus at 11:42 PM and got back at 8:46 AM." No accusation. No raised eyebrow. Just data, said in passing. That casualness IS the message: being watched is normal here. And notice Mae's response. Not anger. Apology. That tells you how tilted the power already is.

Empathy that extracts. Mae mentions her father's illness. The tone softens: "I'm so sorry." Then the pivot: "Did you reach out to any Circlers?" Even grief must be shared with the company. This is not care. It is enforcement wearing care's clothing.

The real power move

In the whole scene, nobody ever says you must. They say "it's all connected," "we're a community," "this is how people know you." Mae is never ordered to comply. She is gently shown that not participating makes you invisible, and in a workplace, invisibility feels dangerous.

Think of it as a velvet rope instead of a locked door. You are not forced in. You are guided.

Translate the velvet rope

Four lines from the film. All of them sound warm. For each one, name what the line is actually doing. This is the exact skill Quiz 2 loves: hearing the move underneath the manners.

Line 1. "You have time? This will just take a minute." (said while already standing at Mae's screen, mid-task)

This is why culture and communication live in the same course. Culture does its heaviest enforcement between the lines. A manager who cannot hear it will end up enforcing things they never meant to say.

Part 7 · The Playbook

Using the lens in real life

Culture reading is not just for film week. It starts paying off immediately, in two situations:

When you are the candidate 🔎

Interviews run both directions. The company is reading you, and now you can read it back. Two questions to ask, out loud:

"Tell me about the last time something went wrong on this team. What happened next?" You are testing the reaction to bad news, the purest culture signal there is.

"When did someone last change a decision by disagreeing?" You are reading the voice gauge.

Then watch the artifacts on the tour: who talks, who stays quiet, what the space enforces. Remember: one morning of watching told you everything about two cafés.

When you become the manager 🧰

What actually moves a culture: change what you measure and reward. React well in the hard moments. Model it yourself. Promote the people who live it. Build rituals that repeat it.

What only decorates a culture: posters, slogans, launch events, mandatory fun.

The whole unit in one line: culture changes when behavior and consequences change, and only when they stay changed long enough to become the stories people tell.

You inherit a silent team

The punchline of the whole unit

The silence was never a personality problem or a vocabulary problem. It was a consequences problem. Somewhere in this team's past, speaking up got punished, and the culture recorded it.

So the only lever that reaches the bottom of the iceberg is the one that rewrites consequences: your reactions and your rewards, repeated until they become new stories. Posters can support that change. Hiring can refresh it. Neither can replace it. When the stories about you change, the culture has changed.

The whole unit, one line each

The four color words

SEE · Artifacts: what you can watch and hear. Offices, rituals, slogans, behavior.

SAY · Espoused values: what the company claims in words.

DO · Enacted behavior: what actually happens. Reactions, rewards, habits.

BELIEVE · Underlying assumptions: the unspoken rules everyone just knows.

Culture lives in the space between SAY and DO. People always believe DO.

The people

Edgar Schein · The iceberg: artifacts, espoused values, underlying assumptions. Also socialization and the leader's embedding signals. Organizational Culture and Leadership.

Marvin Bower (1960s) · "The way we do things around here." Made famous by Deal & Kennedy (1982).

Irving Janis (1972) · Groupthink: tight groups protecting agreement instead of truth.

Amy Edmondson · Psychological safety: speaking up is safe here. The antidote to strength without voice, and the Project Aristotle finding from Week 1.

Groysberg, Lee, Price & Cheng (2018) · A healthy culture quietly aligns people, and leaders shape it constantly.

Elon Musk / Twitter→X (2022-23) · Live case: one public reaction to dissent rewrote a culture in days.

Howard Schultz / Starbucks · Live case: benefits, "partners," and modeled compassion built a belonging culture over decades. The promise still gets tested.

Wells Fargo (2016-20) · Live case: targets + fear beat the values poster. ~3.5M unauthorized accounts, 5,300 fired, $3B settlement.

The Circle (novel 2013, film 2017) · The lab specimen: a strong culture using soft power to ask for everything.

Key terms, one line each

Organizational culture · the shared values, beliefs, and unwritten rules that shape how people actually behave.

Espoused vs. enacted gap · the distance between the poster and the meeting. People believe the meeting.

Socialization · how newcomers absorb the culture. It starts before day one.

Culture carriers · stories, rituals, symbols, language, heroes, space, rewards, taboos.

Embedding signals · the leader's five channels: measure, react, reward, model, hire and fire.

Strong culture · values widely shared and deeply held. Fast and aligned, but conformity-prone.

Groupthink · agreement protected instead of truth.

Psychological safety · dissent is safe. Strength's necessary partner.

Normative control · rules enforced by belonging instead of policy. Nothing to appeal, so people police themselves.

Fit hiring · selecting for culture match. Protective, and one step from hiring for sameness.

You made it through 🧊

Seven checkpoints, six experiments, two cafés, one bank, one very warm dystopia, and one big idea: every organization runs an invisible rulebook, and you now know how to read it.

The whole unit in eight lines
  1. Culture is the invisible rulebook: what people actually do, not what the handbook says. One morning of watching can reveal it.
  2. It runs three layers deep: what you SEE, what the company SAYS, what everyone BELIEVES without saying (Schein's iceberg).
  3. The gap between SAY and DO is the most honest data in any building. People believe consequences, not posters.
  4. Culture grows from founders, old wins, and scars. It travels through carriers: stories, rituals, symbols, language, heroes, space, rewards, taboos.
  5. Leaders broadcast culture through five signals: what they measure, react to, reward, model, and who they hire and fire. Twitter/X rewrote its rulebook in one public week. Starbucks spent decades building the opposite one.
  6. Strong cultures buy alignment and speed, and can silence dissent. Wells Fargo is the bill for strength without voice. The target: strong plus psychologically safe.
  7. The Circle shows culture as soft control: a fit-test interview, rituals and chants, moral reframing, praise as pressure, and "optional" that is not. The velvet rope beats the locked door.
  8. To change a culture, change behavior and consequences until the stories change. Posters are wallpaper. Reactions are law.
